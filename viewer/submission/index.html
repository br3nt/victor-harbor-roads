Submission: Victor Harbor Integrated Transport Strategy (preliminary Vision)

To: City of Victor Harbor (localgov@victor.sa.gov.au)
From: Brent Jacobs, Encounter Bay (resident and ratepayer)
Date: 22 November 2026

I am an Encounter Bay resident and ratepayer, and I have completed the survey. Because the consultation offered no modelling of what the proposed changes would do, I built a traffic simulation of Victor Harbor myself, using SUMO (Simulation of Urban Mobility), widely used open-source traffic simulation software that moves every vehicle through the road network second by second. It is built from publicly available data: the Australian Bureau of Statistics population figures, OpenStreetMap roads, the Department for Infrastructure and Transport's traffic counts, Council's own 2017 counts and Council's growth scenarios. I then tested Council's options, and some alternatives, in it on a school-term weekday and a summer peak day, now and as the town grows. The views in this submission are based on the results of those simulations, together with a review of the documents behind the Strategy.

I support planning for growth, better public transport, walking and cycling. I do not support the town-centre package as presented (the one-way loop, a blanket 30 km/h limit, shared zones and new signals), and I ask Council to test options properly before asking residents to rate them.

My submission also includes a short presentation of the findings, attached to my email and available at https://br3nt.github.io/victor-harbor-roads/deck/. The full evidence, hotspot by hotspot, is at https://br3nt.github.io/victor-harbor-roads/onboarding/, and animations of each hotspot (today, Council's package and an alternative, side by side) at https://br3nt.github.io/victor-harbor-roads/hotspots/. The images below link to the same views in the interactive simulation viewer, and residents can check their own trips at https://br3nt.github.io/victor-harbor-roads/maps/residents/. How the simulation was built, and where every piece of data comes from, is set out at the end.

1. The town-centre package makes traffic worse, for no traffic gain

In the simulation, with traffic built from 2025 data:

  • On a summer peak day the package adds about 28 seconds to the average trip and about 60% more time stopped in queues across the town (about 275 more vehicle-hours a day). On a school-term weekday: about 14 seconds a trip and 58% more queueing.
  • On a summer day the one-way loop is the largest single cause; on a term weekday the signals are.
  • Most single trips change by seconds. The cost lands on particular streets and at particular times, below.

2. It hurts specific streets and people

  • Trucks on the foreshore: on a summer day about 80 trucks a day (about 15 today) take Bridge Terrace and the north end of Flinders Parade into Coral Street under the package, and about 160 with the loop on its own. All traffic on that stretch roughly doubles (about 2,600 to 5,200 vehicles a day).
  • Residential side streets: drivers take the fastest route, so slowing the centre pushes traffic into side streets. Acraman Street carries about three times today's traffic, and Lindsay Street residents wait about 80 seconds instead of 15 to get onto Crozier Road. Acraman Street already has crash hotspots. Back streets near the town-centre service station and Inman/Newland Streets see waits roughly double.
  • Everyday summer trips: Encounter Bay to a supermarket about a minute longer; McCracken to medical services typically 1–1.5 minutes longer.

3. It was never tested or costed

  • The only technical report, SMEC's Phase 1 report (Revision 2, August 2026), contains no one-way loop and no 30 km/h proposal. Its model is described as a simplified model of a typical weekday, and only a 'Do Nothing' network was run. Testing, modelling and costings are scheduled for February–June 2027, after this consultation.
  • At the 26 October 2026 meeting the Strategy was discussed for about five minutes. No speaker mentioned the loop, 30 km/h or the signals, and the cost estimates one councillor asked for are not in the consultation material.
  • The newest detailed town-centre counts are from April 2017, taken in school holidays. SMEC's daily volumes for Hindmarsh Road and Torrens Street are roughly half to two-thirds of the Department for Infrastructure and Transport's (DIT) published figures.
  • DIT's Speed Limit Guideline (version 3, October 2023) says a 30 or 40 km/h limit “is not intended for streets with short holiday or tourism peaks”, that freight and major traffic routes “are unsuitable”, and that 30 km/h suits roads designed for it with alternative routes available. Torrens Street and Hindmarsh Road are State roads and DIT freight routes, and only DIT can approve speed limits.

4. Cheaper, targeted fixes do better

The simulation shows where the delay actually is: drivers waiting to turn right out of side streets onto single-lane main roads around the middle of the day (Victoria Street near the shops, side streets off Hindmarsh Road, the Bridge Terrace / Grantley Avenue link) and, as the town grows, Crozier Road and Greenhills Road joining Armstrong Road. It is not the town-centre roundabouts, which mostly wait 3–8 seconds.

  • Turn lanes on Crozier Road at Armstrong Road, plus a roundabout at Hindmarsh Road / Grantley Avenue: about 11% less time stopped on a term weekday and 18% less on a summer day, now.
  • Roundabouts at Armstrong Road's junctions with Crozier Road and Greenhills Road, plus Grantley: about 24% less in 2041 (about 15 years, high growth), where the Council's package adds about 60%.
  • These are rough layouts tested in a model, not designs or costings, but they show where effort should go.

5. The ring road: keep it, and fix its junctions

The consultation asks residents to choose between a new outer freight ring road (Option 1) and keeping the current ring road along Welch Road and Armstrong Road (Option 2). Because Council's map is only indicative, I modelled Option 1 as described in two plausible alignments: about 10 km from Inman Valley Road to Victor Harbor Road using existing roads and short new links, with the existing ring lowered to 60 km/h. At the State's high-growth figure in 2041 (about 15 years) on a school-term weekday:

  • Option 1 makes queueing worse: about 18–30% more time stopped than today's roads. Slowing the existing ring pushes traffic onto Hindmarsh Road, Adelaide Road and the town centre, and trucks on town-centre streets rise by about 28%.
  • Option 2 with junction fixes does best: keeping the ring and adding roundabouts at its Crozier Road and Greenhills Road junctions (with the Grantley roundabout) cuts time stopped by about 24%.
  • Widening the existing ring to four lanes on its own makes things worse (about 23% more) unless those junctions are fixed, because drivers from Crozier Road then wait almost twice as long to join it.

Option 1 is also by far the most expensive. Council should publish its own alignment and modelling before asking residents to choose.

See it in the viewer: Option 1 compared with today's roads, 2041, term weekday, 12:30; Option 2 with junction fixes.

6. Growth is not solved by any option tested

At the State's high-growth figure, by 2041 (about 15 years) queueing on a school-term weekday is about four times today's on today's roads, and on a summer afternoon demand exceeds what the network can carry whichever option is built. The model places the new suburbs on today's roads, without their future streets and link roads, so this shows the size of the problem rather than the answer. That is the reason to model the whole network, including those future roads, before committing to any one piece.

What I ask Council to do

  • Publish the model and the data (counts, calibration, assumptions) and have them peer reviewed.
  • Model every option, including summer peak and event days, against both growth scenarios, and publish the results before endorsing a Vision.
  • Collect current counts: term-time and summer turning counts at the town-centre intersections, and permanent counters on Torrens Street and Hindmarsh Road.
  • Trial before committing: temporary changes through known busy periods, with before and after data and success criteria published in advance.
  • Put alternatives on the table: keep two-way streets; targeted turn lanes and roundabouts where the delay actually is, staged with growth; time- or event-based speed limits on variable signs instead of a permanent 30 km/h.
  • Publish cost estimates for each element, as a councillor asked on 26 October.
  • Settle DIT's position on speed limits and the freight routes before options go back to the public.

How the simulation was built, and how sure the results are

The simulation uses SUMO (Simulation of Urban Mobility), open-source traffic software that moves every car, truck and caravan through the road network second by second. Trips are generated from where people live and the shops, schools, services and beaches they travel to; summer adds visitor traffic. Each option is the same day's trips on a changed road network, so differences show what the change itself does. It is partly calibrated: in the town centre it carries about 80% of the counted traffic, so town-centre delays are more likely understated than overstated. Results are best read as differences between options, not exact times. Everything is published so it can be checked:

  • Method: simulation design and all results.
  • Data and where it comes from: the sources register lists every document and dataset with its publisher, date and an archived checksum.
  • Forecasting: population and growth, and how the 2017 counts were brought forward to 2025 and checked against newer counts.
  • Every assumption: method and assumptions, one page per topic (road network, trips, counts and calibration, measures, the options, the ring road).
  • The code, so anyone can rerun it: github.com/br3nt/victor-harbor-roads.
